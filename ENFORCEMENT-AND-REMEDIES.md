# Enforcement, Financial Remedies, and Recovery Funding

Status: working design for legal and humanitarian review  
Version: 0.1-draft  
Date: 4 August 2026

This document turns the framework's commitment to effective remedy into an enforcement model. It is not an operative fee schedule, does not create a debt, and is not legal advice.

## 1. Objective

Enforcement should make harmful, deliberate, concealed, repeated, or commercially gainful exploitation economically unattractive; preserve evidence; stop continuing misuse; restore creator control; compensate lost use value, investment, opportunity, and other recoverable loss; prevent an exploiter retaining attributable gains; fund access to justice; and repair harm to affected people. The creator is an affected person. A wrongdoer should not externalise the supported costs of production, investigation, protection, representation, enforcement, and remediation onto the creator, victims, contributors, or the public.

The first priority in an emergency is peace, safety, cessation, evidence, and effective human remedy. That does not make the creator's material interests optional. Death, serious injury, trafficking, slavery, violence, and destruction of agency cannot be priced into permission, but payment may compensate loss, remove illicit gain, deter repetition, and sustain enforcement. The framework must classify deterrent, exemplary, additional, enhanced, punitive, recovery-contingent, or bounty-like mechanisms by their real legal basis rather than rejecting them by vocabulary alone.

The framework therefore distinguishes:

1. copyright and other statutory remedies determined under law;
2. ordinary contract damages for a party who accepted a contract;
3. proportionate agreed compensation or liquidated damages supported by a contemporaneous pre-estimate or legitimate-interest analysis;
4. reasonable, documented enforcement and collection costs recoverable only to the extent law, contract, or a court permits;
5. a settlement or retroactive-licence price that becomes payable only if accepted; and
6. an external enforcement-funding arrangement with lawyers, investigators, insurers, or funders.

## 2. The non-assenter rule

An excluded user or other non-licensee cannot ordinarily be made a contractual debtor solely because a public notice declares a fee. If there is no proven contract, the rights holder should pursue the remedies applicable to unauthorised use and may offer settlement. The notice should say:

> No licence is granted to an ineligible or excluded actor. Unauthorised dealing with protected material may infringe copyright or other rights. The Rights Holder reserves all remedies available under applicable law, including injunctive relief, damages or an account of profits where available, additional damages where awarded, and recoverable costs. Any stated settlement or retroactive-licence amount is an offer unless and until accepted or ordered; it is not an adjudicated debt.

This preserves a serious claim without misrepresenting its legal status.

## 3. Enforcement tracks

### Track A — accepted licensee

Determine whether the event is a covenant breach, an act outside the IP grant, or both. Use notice and cure unless urgent protection is necessary. Available claims may include contractual loss, valid agreed compensation, termination, injunction, copyright remedies for unlicensed acts, interest on an established debt, and recoverable costs.

### Track B — excluded or non-assenting actor

Do not issue a contractual debt demand. Preserve evidence, send an accurate rights notice, request cessation and preservation of records, and assess injunction, copyright damages or account of profits, additional damages, conversion/detention, access-control, platform, tort, regulatory, or other remedies. A settlement offer may include a prospective or retroactive licence only if granting one is consistent with safety and project policy.

### Track C — affected-person harm

Where use involves trafficking, forced labour, exploitation, violence, serious injury, privacy harm, discrimination, remedy evasion, or other illegality, prioritise immediate safety, cessation, evidence preservation, identification of principals and operators, competent authorities, victim support, restoration, and guarantees of non-recurrence. Then pursue available statutory, contractual, civil, regulatory, or criminal pathways and supported enforcement and remediation costs. A private licence fee must not purchase permission to continue a prohibited harm.

Deliberate gain, concealment, recurrence, destruction of evidence, use of intermediaries to avoid responsibility, targeting of vulnerable people, and continued conduct after notice should be recorded as potential aggravating facts where the applicable law makes them relevant. The licence must not claim power to determine criminal guilt or impose a private criminal fine.

### Track D — mistaken allegation or protected act

Pause collection, preserve the response, and conduct independent human review. Restore access or permission where appropriate. A statutory exception, non-infringing independent creation, incorrect identity match, or lack of evidence must not be converted into settlement pressure.

## 4. Financial components

### 4.1 Court-controlled copyright remedies

In Australia, section 115 of the Copyright Act 1968 permits injunctions and either damages or an account of profits. Additional damages are discretionary and may reflect flagrancy, deterrence, post-infringement conduct, benefit, and other relevant matters. The generator may reserve these remedies but must not predetermine what a court will award or recover damages and profits twice for the same infringement.

### 4.2 Ordinary contractual loss

For an accepted agreement, claim loss according to governing law and evidence. The record may include lost licence value, investigation and incident-response costs, remediation, operational disruption, affected-person support, and other causally connected loss. Avoid remote, duplicated, speculative, or expressly unrecoverable items.

### 4.3 Agreed compensation or liquidated damages

The generator may produce an agreed-compensation schedule only when all of the following are supplied:

- the protected legitimate interest;
- why loss would be difficult to quantify when contracting;
- a reasonable valuation method and supporting evidence;
- event-specific components, assumptions, range, and cap;
- proportionality to the interest and foreseeable consequences;
- avoidance of duplicate recovery;
- a review date and change procedure;
- a savings clause for mandatory law; and
- jurisdiction-specific penalty and unfair-contract-term review.

A defensible calculation model is:

`Agreed compensation = supported use-value component + supported response-cost component + supported remediation component`

The model should use documented values, not a multiplier selected merely to frighten or reward enforcement. A higher amount may sometimes protect interests beyond direct cost, but it requires an evidence-backed legitimate-interest analysis. Calling a sum a “licence fee” or “liquidated damages” does not prevent a court from examining its substance.

### 4.4 Enforcement and debt-collection costs

A generated term should be limited to reasonable and documented costs actually and properly incurred because of the breach, recoverable only to the extent permitted by governing law or court order. It should not promise every solicitor-client cost, internal overhead, commission, or collection charge regardless of necessity or proportionality.

Collection activity begins only after a debt has a valid basis. Disputed allegations must be identified as disputed. Agents must be instructed to comply with applicable consumer, privacy, professional-conduct, and debt-collection rules; the rights holder may be responsible for its agent's conduct.

### 4.5 Interest

Interest should apply only to an amount that has become due under an accepted contract, settlement, judgment, or law. Use a transparent reference rate plus a reasonable margin, a non-compounding default unless counsel approves otherwise, the correct accrual date, and any statutory cap. Do not use interest as a disguised penalty.

### 4.6 Settlement and retroactive licence

The rights holder may publish a method for making settlement offers, but not a declaration that every alleged infringer owes the result. The offer should identify:

- the alleged acts and evidence;
- denied and disputed matters;
- the statutory and contractual bases relied on;
- cessation, deletion, audit, attribution, remediation, and future-use terms;
- the proposed monetary amount and calculation;
- whether any retroactive or future permission is safe and legally available;
- a response period without coercion;
- independent advice and dispute options; and
- when, if accepted, the settlement becomes binding.

Repeated, unsafe, concealed, or excluded use need not be offered a licence. Settlement cannot authorise unlawful conduct.

## 5. Enforcement funding and recovery-contingent incentives

The framework expressly supports recovery-contingent incentives where they are lawful because a right that an individual cannot afford to enforce is often only theoretical. A licence cannot by itself retain a law practice or funder, so the operative fee, funding, agency, referral, insurance, or rights-management arrangement must be made through a separate agreement satisfying the professional and funding rules of the relevant jurisdiction.

For example, the Legal Profession Uniform Law (NSW) permits some conditional costs agreements with an uplift fee, subject to conditions and a 25% cap on the legal costs excluding disbursements for litigious matters, while section 183 prohibits a law practice's fee being calculated by reference to an award, settlement, or recovered property. Other jurisdictions and litigation-funding arrangements differ.

A project enforcement programme may use:

- a ring-fenced enforcement reserve;
- legal-expense insurance;
- a lawyer-approved conditional costs agreement;
- a compliant third-party litigation funder;
- fixed or staged fees for investigators and technical experts;
- a carefully reviewed recovery-contingent enforcement or referral reward—sometimes described commercially as a bounty—payable only from verified recovery under a separate agreement; and
- costs orders and recoveries when actually available.

The creator must retain informed control over whether to license, settle, continue, or discontinue a claim, subject to applicable professional duties, funding terms, and court supervision. No referral, funder, or investigator payment should reward accusation alone, harassment, unauthorised practice, evidence tampering, privacy invasion, conflicted advice, inflated claims, or settlement contrary to creator instructions and affected-person rights.

## 6. Recovery waterfall

Unless governing law, a court, affected-person rights, insurance, funding terms, or a settlement requires another order, project policy should consider allocating net recoveries in this sequence:

1. urgent protection, restoration, and remedy for affected natural persons, expressly including the creator;
2. creator/rightsholder restitution for lost use value, proven loss, impaired investment, diverted opportunity, and attributable gain;
3. reasonable protective, investigative, legal, enforcement, funding, collection, and remediation costs;
4. unpaid obligations to contributors directly connected with the exploited work;
5. replenishment of a creator-controlled enforcement and continuity reserve; and
6. only then, other adopted project purposes.

“Net recovery” must be defined and transparently accounted for. The waterfall does not create a third party's legal entitlement unless an operative agreement or law expressly does so.

## 7. Evidence record

Before asserting money, record:

- ownership and standing;
- the Work, release, file, and licence hashes;
- contract formation and acceptance evidence, if relied on;
- the actor, principal, operators, and authority chain;
- each alleged act, place, date, duration, scale, and purpose;
- protected-act and grant-boundary analysis;
- notices and post-notice conduct;
- revenue, benefit, avoided licence value, loss, response cost, and remediation evidence;
- affected persons and protective needs;
- applicable exceptions, defences, limitations, and countervailing rights;
- evidence provenance, access controls, retention, and chain of custody; and
- independent reviewer, decision, reasons, appeal, and outcome.

An automated classifier may flag a matter but must not issue a legal demand or determine liability without accountable human review.

## 8. Procedural safeguards

Every generated procedure must include:

- intelligible notice of the rule, facts, claimant, legal basis, and amount;
- a reasonable opportunity to respond and correct identity or evidence;
- a cure path where compatible with urgent safety and continuing-infringement needs;
- preservation of urgent injunctive or protective relief;
- proportionality and consistency review;
- protection against retaliation and abusive collection;
- an accessible review or dispute channel;
- rules for confidential, personal, and victim information; and
- correction, withdrawal, restoration, and audit when an allegation fails.

## 9. Generator rejection rules

The generator must reject any profile that disables creator autonomy or lacks a real legal basis. It sends deterrent or recovery-contingent mechanisms to mandatory review rather than erasing them. A profile fails when it:

- treats public inspection, technical access, or an exploiter's resources as permission;
- prevents the creator refusing or conditioning an ungranted use;
- omits creator investment, economic-interest, profit-recovery, cost-recovery, and enforcement-funding analysis;
- uses “punitive”, “fine”, “bounty”, “additional”, “enhanced”, or “deterrent” language without accurately classifying the legal basis, decision-maker, formation stage, evidence, legitimate interests, proportionality, and payable event;
- treats prohibited use as acceptance of a contract;
- declares a non-assenter's unadjudicated amount to be a debt;
- uses a large arbitrary multiplier with no valuation or legitimate-interest evidence;
- requires damages and an account of profits cumulatively for the same loss;
- recovers legal or collection costs regardless of reasonableness and legal permission;
- allows a collector to misstate, harass, coerce, or continue pressing a genuinely disputed debt without review;
- promises a lawyer a recovery percentage without professional-rules review;
- lets payment buy permission for harmful or unlawful conduct;
- omits affected-person remedy, notice, response, evidence, review, or mistake correction; or
- automates final liability or enforcement without an accountable natural person.
